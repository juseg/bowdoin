% preliminary abstract
\begin{abstract}

    All around the margins of Greenland and Antarctica, marine-terminating
    outlet glaciers, the fastest-flowing on Earth, funnel ice flow from vast
    regions of the ice sheet interiors towards the ocean. While it is well
    known that the buoyancy of these partly submerged glacier tongues yields
    reduced friction, and hence faster ice velocities, quantifying the precise
    interplay between basal sliding and internal deformation in these remote,
    inaccessible, and often highly crevassed environments has been
    challenging. Yet this tight balance between buoyant forces, basal friction
    and internal viscous stresses is precisely the gatekeeper to further mass
    loss from the ice sheets.
    %
    Here, we present a three-year record of tilt from sensors frozen 123 to 265
    metres deep into Bowdoin Glacier, a tidewater glacier in northwest
    Greenland, and compare it to surface velocity records from geopositioning
    and satellite remote sensing. These records show that the glacier motion is
    largely dominated by sliding, while the much smaller englacial shear
    deformation follows near-linear viscous creep. Internal deformation speeds
    up alongside sliding at tidal \todo{to be confirmed in crosscorr figure}
    to seasonal time scales, resulting in a near-constant ratio of
    twenty-to-one between basal sliding and internal
    shear deformation over the whole measurement period.

\end{abstract}
