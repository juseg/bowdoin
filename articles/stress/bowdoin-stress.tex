\begin{abstract}

    \note{abstract from ISAR-8.}
    %
    Englacial stress, the elusive variable governing glacier motion, has rarely
    been measured in situ. Instead, our empirical understanding of ice dynamics
    largely relies on laboratory flow-law experiments, but field measurements
    of stress-induced glacier surface velocity and englacial tilt indicate that
    crystal orientation, molten ice fraction and impurities may complicate the
    application of laboratory-derived laws in nature.
    %
    Here, we present a three-year stress record from piezometers frozen 123 to
    265 metres deep into the Bowdoin tidewater glacier in Greenland. These
    sensors were not initially intended to measure solid stress, but instead to
    locate instruments in hotwater-drilled boreholes. However, they continued
    to record pressure changes after the complete refreezing of the boreholes
    and the stabilisation of ice temperatures well below the melting point.
    %
    All sensors recorded in-phase stress variations with 12-hour, 24-hour and
    14-day periodicities, revealing a tidal signal in winter, disturbed during
    independently documented speed-up events in summer. The signal shows
    amplitudes of one to four kilopascals, only an order of magnitude weaker
    than the two metres tidal amplitude measured at sea. However, stress
    measurements are anticorrelated with the tide, and show a delay of one to
    two hours, so that maximum stresses occur a little after low tide. While
    detailed interpretations are hampered by to the lack of calibration, our
    data indicate that direct stress measurements in glaciers are feasible.

\end{abstract}

\section{Methods}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Bowdoin Glacier}

    Bowdoin Glacier is an outlet tidewater glacier of the Greenland Ice Sheet.
    It is likely fully grounded. The velocity is modulated by the seasons,
    weather, daily and tidal cycles. A lot of research has been done.

\subsection{Drilling sites}

    Three boreholes were drilled 2\,km from the calving front in summer 2014,
    and instrumented for up to three years as they flowed downstream by 1\,km.
    Records were progressively interrupted by instrument failures until final
    retrieval in July 2017.

\subsection{Instrumentation}

    BH1 and BH3 were equipped with ETHZ-built digital inclinometer units
    (DIBOSS), containing several sensors, including top-mounted Keller pressure
    sensors X-series 7LX, calibrated by the manufacturer, and protected under a
    silicon membrane (Fig.~\ref{fig:bores}; Claudia PhD p.~43). These sensors
    were intended to locate the units vertically but continued to record after
    refreezing (as recorded by thermistors), while remaining within 6--19° of
    the vertical (as shown by inclinometers). Hence we refer to their record as
    (subvertical) stress.

\subsection{Pituffik tide}

    These records are compared to tidal data from Pituffik from the Technical
    University of Denmark (DTU), provided within the Global Sea Level Observing
    System network (GLOSS). A pressure sensor was also installed for a short
    period in Bowdoin fjord showing comparable tidal phase and magnitude to
    that measured at Pituffik, hence the Pituffik record is representative of
    tidal fluctuations in Bowdoin Fjord. Tidal height was converted to pressure
    using standard sea water density.

\subsection{Spectral analysis}

    The date of borehole closure is estimated when temperature reach 80\% of
    their minimum value, relative to 0\,°C, over the measuring period
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:bores}). We conduct spectral analysis on the Bowdoin stress
    post-closure and Pituffik tide records, including Fast Fourier Transform,
    rolling-window spectrograms, continuous wavelet transforms, band-pass
    filtering, cross-correlation, and moving-window cross-correlation. The
    sensors use a variable sample rate depending on available solar energy,
    and some analyses apply resampling (detailed in captions). Alternative
    methods may be presented in supplement?

\section{Results}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -
\subsection{Stress timeseries}
% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -

    Before borehole closure, the piezometers record water table fluctuations
    within each borehole (Fig.~\ref{fig:nofil}a). During borehole closure,
    there is a strong peak in pressure. After borehole closure, several signals
    are visible. On the yearly scale, some sensors show a tendency for higher
    stress in the summer months. On a weekly scale, several sensors show
    negative stress peaks during the melting seasons (probably corresponding to
    glacier speed-up events, see discussion). Zooming-in to daily and hourly
    scales, all sensors show distinct 12-hourly oscillations, with amplitudes
    an order of magnitude below Pituffik tides (Fig.~\ref{fig:nofil}b and~c).
    Thermistors inside the casing document the timing of borehole closure
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:nofil}d).

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -
\subsection{Spectral content}
% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -

    Fourier transforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:pgram}) of the resampled signal reveal a
    frequency spectrum very similar to that of regional Pituffik tides, with
    predominant frequencies in the 12-hour band (tidal constituents
    $\mathrm{S_2}$, $\mathrm{M_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$), and second-degree
    frequencies in the 24-hour band ($\mathrm{K_1}$, $\mathrm{O_1}$).
    Lomb-Scargle periodograms (supplement) show similar results.

    Fourier spectrograms (Fig.~\ref{fig:sgram}) reveal that 12-hour periods
    predominate the signal outside the melt season (Oct-May). During the melt
    season (June-Sep) the tidal spectrum is overprinted with noise in all
    tested frequency bands, and stronger signal in the 24-hour band.
    Continuous wavelet transform (Fig.~\ref{fig:wlets}) show that the tidal
    signal remain present during the melt season, but it is overprinted by
    other frequency, particularly in a broad band around the daily frequency
    (hypothesis: especially during speed-up events, see discussion).

% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -
\subsection{Phase relationships}
% -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -- -

    A 4-to-12 hour band-pass filter was applied to isolate tidal signal from
    high-frequency sampling noise, a low-frequency content unrelated to tidal
    processes (Fig.~\ref{fig:lines}). The filter was applied once forward and
    once backward to annulate phase shift. Filtered timeseries show 12-hourly
    amplitude roughly an order of magnitude lower than the tide, and similarly
    modulated by a 14-day period. The frequency band was optimised for high
    cross-correlation in the next step, but other band-pass and high-pass
    windows gave similar results (supplement).

    Cross-correlation between Bowdoin stress and Pituffik tides is first
    showcased on a selected interval of one month were data from all sensors
    remained available, but two sensors close to the glacier bed remained in
    the melt water from the drilling (Fig.~\ref{fig:ccorr}a). The maximum
    absolute is an anti-correlation, observed with a delay of ca.~1--2\,h.
    That is, maximum compressive stress occurs a little after the minimum tide
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:ccorr}b). This phase delay appears to peak around 200\,m
    depth, but the relation between sensor depth and phase delay, if any, is
    not clear (Fig.~\ref{fig:ccorr}c).

    Rolling window cross-correlation (Fig.~\ref{fig:mcorr}) show that this
    phase delay remains stable over much of the measuring period, with the
    exceptions of melt seasons, where the correlation weakens, and the later
    part of the record, where it becomes inconsistent between different sensor
    units (interestingly, UI04 and UI05 display a slow-down and almost complete
    halt in tilting over that period, could this be decoupling from the bed,
    see discussion).
